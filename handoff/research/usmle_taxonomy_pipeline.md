## 2. Step 3 CCS: what a study site must simulate

### Takeaway
Official pages fetched confirm only that CCS occurs on Day 2 and lists covered competencies without weights; mechanics details below come from general knowledge of the Primum CCS software and should be verified against the USMLE CCS tutorial/practice software.

### Cited Findings
- CCS is part of Day 2 (ACM); CCS content and competency tables have coverage checkmarks but no percentages; encounter frames are Initial Workup, Continuing Care, Urgent Intervention — [Step 3 specs](https://www.usmle.org/exam-resources/step-3-materials/step-3-content-outline-and-specifications)
- Step 3 moved to the new delivery software March 10, 2026 — [USMLE](https://www.usmle.org/test-delivery-software-updates-step-2-ck-and-step-1-coming-may-2026)

### Inferences (UNVERIFIED feature list for a simulator, based on Primum CCS as publicly known)
- Free-text order entry against a large order dictionary (thousands of orders, no suggestion lists), with order confirmation; history and focused/complete physical exam selectable by body area, each consuming simulated time.
- Simulated clock with "advance clock" to a time or to next result; results returned with realistic turnaround; patient status updates/interruptions.
- Patient location changes (office, ER, ward, ICU, home) constraining available orders.
- Case ends when the system determines; scoring based on appropriateness, timing, sequence and location of actions, and avoiding harmful/unnecessary orders.
- Product needs: an order dictionary with synonyms, a per-case state machine (vitals trajectory as function of time and orders), a rubric per case (required, beneficial, neutral, harmful actions with time windows), and a debrief screen.

## 3. NBME item-writing guide: authoring checklist for the AI pipeline

### Takeaway
The NBME guide is now in its 6th edition (form-gated free PDF; includes new material on patient characteristics and alternate item types). Its core single-best-answer rules (cover-the-options, homogeneous options, avoid testwiseness and irrelevant-difficulty flaws) translate directly into automatable lint checks.

### Cited Findings
- 6th edition; PDF downloadable after a form; Spanish and Portuguese versions; covers item/vignette writing, psychometrics basics, improving items for foundational and clinical sciences, plus new material on using patient characteristics in items and alternate item types; no terms of use shown on the page — [NBME Item-Writing Guide](https://www.nbme.org/educators/item-writing-guide)
- A 6th ed. (2020) listing exists under the title "Constructing Written Test Questions for the Basic and Clinical Sciences" — [UNM listing](https://cpl.health.unm.edu/AssetListing/Writing-Questions-for-Learning-and-Assessment-2573/Constructing-Written-Test-Questions-For-the-Basic-and-Clinical-Sciences-6th-ed-NBME-2020-5944)
- Rules (from NBME short-format guideline) — [NBME item writing guidelines (short format)](https://aarad.org/assets/Alliances/AMSER/Educator_Resources/Student_Evaluation/2003-item-writing-guidelines-NBME-short-format.pdf):
  - Structure: stem (clinical vignette), lead-in question, options (one key + usually four distractors); long stem, short options.
  - Cover-the-options rule: answerable from stem + lead-in alone.
  - Homogeneous options: same category (all diagnoses/tests/treatments), rankable on one dimension; no double options unless all are double.
  - Testwiseness flaws: grammatical cues; logical cues (exhaustive subsets like increases/decreases/unchanged); absolute terms (always/never); longer/more specific key; word repeats from stem in key; convergence (key shares most elements with other options).
  - Irrelevant difficulty: long/complicated options; inconsistent numeric data (mixed formats, non-ordered, overlapping ranges); vague frequency terms (rarely/usually); non-parallel language/illogical order; "none of the above"; negative stems (EXCEPT/NOT); tricky or overly complex stems; hinged items.
  - Test important/common/serious concepts and application, not isolated recall; avoid true/false and "Which of the following is correct?" stems.

### Inferences — Authoring checklist the pipeline must enforce (machine + human)
1. Vignette order: age/sex, setting, chief complaint, duration, history, PMH/meds/social/family, vitals, exam, labs/imaging (SI + conventional units per official lab table).
2. Lead-in is a closed, single question ("Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"); passes cover-the-options test (LLM judge answers without options, must match key).
3. 5 options (A-E), homogeneous type, alphabetical or logical/numeric order, similar length (flag key if >1.5x mean distractor length).
4. Regex bans: "all of the above", "none of the above", "EXCEPT", "NOT", "always", "never", "rarely", "usually", "frequently".
5. Lexical-overlap check stem vs key (word-repeat cue); convergence check (key should not be the option sharing the most tokens with others).
6. Exactly one defensible best answer; each distractor has a written rationale for why it is wrong.
7. Every item tagged with the taxonomy from Section 1, plus a one-sentence educational objective, plus difficulty target.
8. No hinged items; no patient-characteristic stereotyping (6th ed. emphasis — read guide for specifics).
9. Originality check vs. existing bank and public sources (embedding similarity) to avoid copying NBME/commercial items.

### Gaps
- Full 6th-edition PDF text not read (form-gated); its specific guidance on patient characteristics and alternate item types was not extracted. "All of the above" is not addressed in the short-format guideline fetched, but is universally treated as a flaw in item-writing literature.

## 4. Evidence on AI-generated MCQs and review/psychometric workflow

### Takeaway
LLM-generated MCQs can match human items on clarity/relevance and roughly on discrimination, but are easier, skew to lower-order recall, and carry factual-error rates of several percent; every study recommends expert review. A defensible pipeline is AI draft -> automated lint -> dual expert review -> pilot -> psychometric retirement/revision.

### Cited Findings
- Prospective cohort (Hong Kong emergency medicine, Aug 2024, ChatGPT-4o, 100 AI vs 100 human MCQs, 24 examinees): AI items easier (difficulty index 0.78 vs 0.69, p<0.01); discrimination similar (0.22 vs 0.26); AI had more factual errors (6% vs 4%), irrelevant items (6% vs 0%), inappropriate difficulty (14% vs 1%); AI mostly lower-order thinking (p=0.003); 24.5 vs 96 person-hours; authors conclude expert review remains necessary — [Law et al., BMC Med Educ 2025 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/de53e05697e64cf089061ab76fe225c3)
- Systematic review & network meta-analysis (PLOS ONE 2026, 5 LLMs vs humans): ChatGPT-4 MCQs of similar quality to human-written on relevance, clarity, distractor quality (very low certainty evidence); Llama 2 worse on clarity (MD -1.21) and distractor quality (MD -1.50); ChatGPT-3.5 worse than both — [Riehm et al., PLOS ONE 2026](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0340277.html)
- Image-based USMLE sample items: GPT-4 73.4% vs GPT-4o 89.5% accuracy on 38 items; higher error rate on Step 2 CK items; authors say expert validation is essential and AI MCQs need review for clinical validity, cognitive appropriateness and pedagogical alignment — [BMC Med Educ 2025 (PMC12374324)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12374324)
- Additional relevant sources found but not read: [Validity of AI-generated MCQs in medical education: a systematic review](https://avesis.gazi.edu.tr/yayin/52dbfdc2-52c2-48ad-b326-82a699de2e7f/validity-of-ai-generated-multiple-choice-questions-in-medical-education-a-systematic-review); [MCQG-SRefine (iterative self-critique for USMLE-style generation, arXiv 2410.13191)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13191); [NUS "Hidden pitfalls in AI-generated MCQs"](https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/taps/?p=73257); [LLMs for generating medical examinations: systematic review](https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/large-language-models-for-generating-medical-examinations-systema/)

### Inferences — recommended workflow
- Stage 1 Generation: LLM given taxonomy cell + learning objective + source-of-truth references (guidelines); asked for higher-order (diagnosis -> mechanism / next step) items to counter the documented lower-order bias.
- Stage 2 Automated QA: NBME lint (Section 3), independent LLM "solver" answering blind (with and without options) — disagreement flags ambiguity; citation check for each factual claim; lab values checked against official table.
- Stage 3 Dual expert review: two US-trained/board-relevant physicians (or one physician + one trained item editor) independently rate accuracy, single best answer, cognitive level, flaws; adjudicate disagreements. Budget for ~5-15% rejection/major revision based on the 6% factual-error and 14% difficulty-mismatch rates above.
- Stage 4 Psychometrics after N responses (common practice; specific thresholds not sourced in this session): p-value (proportion correct) target ~0.30-0.85; point-biserial/discrimination >=0.20 acceptable, <0.10 or negative -> review key; distractor analysis (each distractor chosen by >=5%, negative point-biserial). Typical minimum N before acting ~100-200 responses.
- Track item version history and reviewer sign-off (audit trail) for defensibility.

### Gaps
- PubMed/PMC pages were captcha-blocked; no 2025-2026 Claude-specific USMLE item-generation study was retrieved. Psychometric thresholds above are standard practice but not cited from a source in this session.

## 5. Learning-science and product-design evidence; Qbank best practices

### Takeaway
FSRS substantially outperforms SM-2 at predicting recall on a ~10k-user Anki dataset, making it the right default scheduler; retrieval practice and interleaving underpin Qbank design, but primary learning-science papers were not retrieved in this session.

### Cited Findings
- SRS benchmark: 9,999 Anki collections, ~350M reviews after filtering (~727M in full dataset); FSRS-6 (with recency weighting) has lower log loss than Anki SM-2 for 99.6% of users; FSRS-6 beats FSRS-5 for 88.2%; caveat that SM-2 was not designed to predict probabilities — [Expertium SRS Benchmark](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)

### Inferences
- Use open-source FSRS (open-spaced-repetition project, MIT-licensed implementations in several languages; license not verified here) for flashcards and for re-queuing missed Qbank items.
- Qbank features commonly cited as UWorld-style strengths (not verified against sources in this session): full explanations covering why each distractor is wrong, tables/figures/illustrations, a boxed "Educational Objective," performance by system/discipline/competency mirroring the official axes, percentile rank vs peers, timed/tutor modes, blueprint-weighted custom blocks, and linked readings. The 2026 nutrition score-report category suggests adding Nutrition as a performance dimension.
- Interleaved, mixed-system blocks (default random) mirror the exam; tutor mode for learning, timed 30-min blocks for simulation.

### Gaps
- No primary sources fetched for retrieval-practice (e.g., Roediger & Karpicke) or interleaving meta-analyses; no direct sourcing of UWorld feature set.

## 6. Terminology/ontology licensing for content tagging

### Takeaway
MeSH is free with attribution; RxNorm's NLM-created names/codes (RXCUI) are public domain but full releases need a free UMLS license; SNOMED CT is free in the US via UMLS but international use (relevant if the business or users are in India) depends on member-country status and may incur fees.

### Cited Findings
- MeSH: no cost, no royalties; must visibly credit NLM and not imply endorsement; redistributors must keep data current or state version — [NLM MeSH Terms & Conditions](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/databases/download/terms_and_conditions_mesh.html)
- RxNorm: NLM-created names and codes (RXCUI) are public domain and may be freely distributed in and outside the US; a free UMLS license is required to download full releases; attribution statement required; no use of NLM name/logo — [RxNorm Terms of Service](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/research/umls/rxnorm/docs/termsofservice.html)
- SNOMED CT: free in the US for UMLS licensees (NLM is a member of IHTSDO/SNOMED International); in non-member countries apply to IHTSDO, fees may apply, and register via MLDS before use; free in member territories, low-income countries, and qualifying research — [NLM SNOMED CT Licensing](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/healthit/snomedct/snomed_licensing.html)
- UMLS license itself is free via UTS sign-up — [NLM UMLS](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/research/umls/quickstart.html)

### Inferences
- Recommended tagging stack: USMLE taxonomy (primary) + MeSH descriptors (free, good for topic linking) + RxNorm RXCUI for drugs (public domain) + ICD-10-CM for diagnoses (US government code set, generally public domain — not verified in this session). Use SNOMED CT only if needed and after checking India's membership status (India is believed to be a SNOMED International member, not verified).

### Gaps
- ICD-10-CM (CDC/NCHS) and ICD-10 (WHO) licensing not fetched; WHO ICD-10 is copyrighted by WHO while ICD-10-CM is a US clinical modification — verify before use.
